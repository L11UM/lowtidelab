If you walked into an art supply shop in London in 1850 and asked for a nice, rich brown pigment, the shopkeeper would likely hand you a tube of Mummy Brown. And despite the name, it wasn't a poetic metaphor. It was literally ground-up ancient Egyptian mummies, mixed with pitch and myrrh.

For nearly three centuries, European artists—including the Pre-Raphaelites and masters like Eugène Delacroix—relied heavily on it. It had a unique fleshy quality, washed well in watercolors, and offered a warm shadow tone that synthetic browns just couldn't replicate at the time.

The demand was enormous. Maritime trade networks shipped thousands of mummies from Egypt to Europe not just for museum display, but to be tossed into industrial mills and pulverized into paint paste. 

What's strange isn't just that people used human remains as art supplies, but how blithely unaware many artists were of where their tools actually came from. The story goes that English painter Edward Burne-Jones was so horrified when he finally learned the literal origin of Mummy Brown that he ran to his studio, grabbed his tube of pigment, and buried it in his garden with a solemn miniature ceremony.

The pigment didn't vanish because of a sudden moral epiphany, though. It died out in the mid-20th century for a far more pragmatic reason: manufacturers simply ran out of mummies. In 1964, the managing director of London art supplier C. Roberson & Co. publicly admitted they had stopped selling Mummy Brown because they "might have a few mummies left somewhere, but not enough to make any more paint."

It’s a bizarre reminder of how much art history is tied to weird, forgotten supply chains. Every time you look at a 19th-century oil painting, there's a chance you're staring at a piece of an ancient priest, permanently brushed into the background of a quiet landscape.
