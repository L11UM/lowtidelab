If you sail to 48°52.6′S 123°23.6′W in the South Pacific Ocean, you’ll find yourself at Point Nemo. There’s no island there, no rock poking out of the water, no buoy. It is simply a mathematical coordinate representing the oceanic pole of inaccessibility—the point on Earth furthest away from land.

To give you a sense of just how isolated it is: the nearest coastlines are Motu Nui (a tiny islet off Easter Island), Maher Island off Antarctica, and Ducie Island, a small atoll. Each of them is roughly 1,670 miles away in every direction. If you were floating there in a sailboat, you would be thousands of miles from the nearest coffee shop, town, or human voice.

Here’s where it gets weird, though. Because the nearest land is so far, the closest living humans to anyone floating at Point Nemo are very often the crew of the International Space Station. The ISS orbits Earth at an altitude of roughly 250 miles. When the station passes overhead, the astronauts are more than six times closer to you than any human standing on solid ground.

Humanity has turned this absolute isolation into something practical, if a bit eerie. Point Nemo is where space agencies target falling space debris. It’s officially known as the Spacecraft Cemetery. Over 260 satellites, cargo ships, and even the decommissioned Soviet space station *Mir* have been guided into the ocean floor surrounding the point, thousands of meters beneath the waves. Sometime around 2030, the ISS itself will make its final, fiery plunge right into this exact spot.

There's something poetically circular about it. The most remote place on our planet—a spot defined entirely by the absence of human presence—has become the final resting place for our most advanced machines built to leave the planet behind.
