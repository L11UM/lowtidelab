Back in the 1990s, an economist named William Nordhaus decided to track the price of light over four thousand years.

He wasn't looking at the price of lamps or electricity bills. He wanted to know the actual cost of a lumen-hour—a unit of raw light output. Specifically, he wanted to measure how much human labor it took to illuminate a dark room for one hour.

His findings were wild. In ancient Babylon, lighting a room for an hour required burning sesame oil that cost about a week’s worth of labor. By 1800, using tallow candles, it took roughly six hours of average work to buy a single day’s worth of basic light. By 1900, with kerosene and early incandescent bulbs, that dropped to about ten minutes. Today, a modern LED gives you that same light for less than a fraction of a single second of work.

Nordhaus calculated that between 1800 and 1990, the real price of light dropped by a factor of somewhere between 3,000 and 10,000.

Here’s the kicker: according to official government data like the Consumer Price Index, the price of light over that exact same 190-year period had only fallen by a factor of three.

Why such a massive gap? Because traditional metrics measure the cost of *inputs*—buying a candle or paying an electric utility—rather than *outcomes*—how much actual illumination you receive. Economic stats treated a dim, smoky wax candle as roughly equivalent to a lightbulb because both were cataloged under "lighting expenses."

This isn't just a quirk of lightbulbs; it's a fundamental issue with how we measure standard of living. Standard economic tools are great at counting dollars spent, but they are notoriously terrible at capturing quality jumps. We constantly undercount how much better life gets because our math tracks the things we buy, not what those things actually do for us.
