Suspended hundreds of meters down in the ocean's twilight zone, *Bathochordaeus stygius* appears less like a solitary animal and more like an architectural installation. The creature itself is a tadpole-shaped solitary tunicate typically measuring under ten centimeters long. Yet it drifts inside a translucent, self-secreted mucus house that can expand over a meter wide.

In the nutrient-limited midwater, organic particles are dispersed and microscopic. *Bathochordaeus stygius* solves this collection problem using a complex, two-stage filtration apparatus. As the animal undulates its muscular tail within an inner chamber, it creates a pump mechanism that draws surrounding seawater through the structure. Coarse outer meshes deflect large detritus that would otherwise clog the system, while an intricate, fine-meshed inner filter concentrates tiny food particles—such as pico- and nanoplankton, detrital fragments, and bacteria—funneling them straight into the animal's mouth.

These delicate structures process seawater at extraordinary rates, sometimes filtering tens of liters per hour. Inevitably, particulate matter clogs the filters. When passage chokes, *B. stygius* simply abandons the entire complex, collapses it, and can inflate a replacement house from specialized cells on its epithelial surface in a matter of hours.

Field research by the [Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute](https://www.mbari.org/animal/giant-larvacean/) reveals that these discarded mucus houses, termed "sinkers," are critical components of the ocean's biological carbon pump. Loaded with trapped carbon, discarded houses can sink at rates exceeding several hundred meters per day. As outlined by [NOAA Ocean Exploration](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/larvacean.html), this rapid descent bypasses the slow breakdown of ordinary marine snow, transporting high-quality organic matter directly to benthic communities on the deep ocean floor before midwater scavengers and microbes can consume it.
