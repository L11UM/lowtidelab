Outside our observation port at 850 meters down the continental slope, a dark, serpentine outline uncoils from the twilight. The frilled shark (*Chlamydoselachus anguineus*) looks less like a modern apex predator and more like an ancient relict, though its distinct family evolved during the Early Jurassic. Reaching lengths of up to two meters, this deep-water specialist inhabits upper continental slopes and submarine canyons, typically at depths between 200 and 1,500 meters.

Its elongated, eel-like body is uniquely adapted for ambush in an energy-poor ecosystem. Unlike most modern sharks, whose mouths sit underneath a protruding snout, *C. anguineus* features a terminal mouth located directly at the tip of its head. Its jaws contain roughly 300 tricuspid (three-pointed) teeth spaced across 25 rows. Each tooth curves backward like a miniature grapple hook. Against the white interior of its mouth, these gleaming prongs attract and trap slippery prey—primarily squid and bony fish—making escape virtually impossible once locked inside.

Because deep-sea survival requires strict energy conservation, the frilled shark lacks the heavy muscular mass needed for high-speed chases. Instead, it relies on a massive liver packed with low-density squalene to maintain near-neutral buoyancy, hovering effortlessly above the ocean floor. Its name derives from six pairs of long, frilly gill slits; the first pair connects fully across the underside of the throat, maximizing gas exchange in oxygen-depleted waters.

Direct behavioral observations remain rare. However, field studies documented by [Smithsonian Ocean](https://ocean.si.edu/ocean-life/fish/frilled-shark) indicate these sharks likely strike by flexing their long cartilaginous trunks and lunging forward like a coiled snake. Comprehensive morphological data from the [Florida Museum of Natural History](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/discover-fish/species-profiles/chlamydoselachus-anguineus/) reveals an exceptionally wide jaw gape, enabling it to swallow prey up to half its own body length. It stands as an extraordinary example of deep-sea evolutionary longevity.
