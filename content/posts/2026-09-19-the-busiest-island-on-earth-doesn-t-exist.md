If you look at the raw data generated by modern software, there is a tiny patch of open ocean in the Gulf of Guinea that looks like the wildest metropolis on Earth.

People log morning jogs across its open waves. Fitness apps record thousands of calories burned directly into the Atlantic. Stolen smartphones ping its coordinates, digital cameras geotag photos there by the millions, and dating profiles routinely insist that someone is looking for love four hundred miles south of Ghana.

This place is known in cartography and developer circles as Null Island. It sits at precisely 0°N, 0°E—the exact intersection of the Prime Meridian and the Equator.

In reality, there is no land there. If you sailed out to visit, you would find sixteen thousand feet of dark water and a solitary, automated weather-monitoring mooring buoy nicknamed Soul, bobbing quietly in the swell.

Null Island exists entirely because of how machines handle confusion. When an app tries to figure out where you are, it queries a geocoding service to convert an address or an IP lookup into latitude and longitude. But if the GPS signal drops, the lookup times out, or a field is left blank, the system has a problem: the database schema demands two numbers.

To a human, "no location" means absence. To a computer, absence is often recorded as `null`. But in hurried code or sloppy data conversions, `null` gets coerced into a zero. 

Suddenly, an error doesn't register as a failure; it registers as valid coordinates: (0, 0). The software shrugs and drops your lost phone, your uploaded photo, or your marathon route straight into the ocean off the African coast.

It’s a funny bug, but it’s also a neat illustration of how software fundamentally misinterprets the physical world. We treat zero as neutral—a blank slate, a non-answer. But the physical world doesn’t have a neutral. You can't stand nowhere without pointing somewhere.
