In 1969, Buzz Aldrin placed a small, suitcase-sized panel on the lunar dust before heading back to the lunar module. It wasn't a flag or a plaque; it was a retroreflector, an array of 100 tiny corner-cube prisms designed to bounce light directly back to wherever it came from.

More than fifty years later, we are still firing lasers at it.

Observatories on Earth regularly aim high-powered pulses of green light at that exact spot on the Moon. Sending a beam through 238,000 miles of atmosphere spreads the light out until it covers a spot several miles wide on the lunar surface. Out of the quadrillions of photons sent in every pulse, usually just *one or two* make the trip back to Earth, arriving about 2.5 seconds later.

It sounds like a ridiculously tedious game of catch, but those few returning particles of light have taught us something remarkable: the Moon is slowly abandoning us.

By measuring the exact round-trip time down to the picosecond, we know the Moon is spiraling away from Earth at a rate of 3.8 centimeters per year—roughly the speed at which human fingernails grow. The culprit is tidal friction. As the Moon pulls on Earth’s oceans, the water creates a slight gravitational drag that pushes the Moon into a higher orbit.

This reveals a strange cosmic coincidence. Right now, the Moon is about 400 times smaller than the Sun, but it also happens to be about 400 times closer. That exact ratio is why total solar eclipses exist—the Moon fits over the Sun like a perfect coin.

In about 600 million years, the Moon will have drifted too far for its shadow to reach Earth completely. Total solar eclipses will become extinct, replaced entirely by ring-of-fire transits.

We happen to live in a fleeting, temporary sweet spot in Earth's history, and the main reason we know its precise speed is because someone left a piece of glass in the dirt half a century ago.
