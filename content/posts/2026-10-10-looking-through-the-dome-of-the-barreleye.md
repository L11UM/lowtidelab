As the *Low Tide Lab* hovers at 650 meters depth off the California coast, our external lights illuminate one of the deep ocean's most extraordinary residents: *Macropinna microstoma*, commonly known as the barreleye fish. Floating nearly motionless in the mesopelagic twilight zone, this small, dark-scaled fish appears unremarkable at first glance—until you look closely at its head.

Covering the upper portion of its head is a delicate, transparent, fluid-filled canopy. Beneath this protective shield sit two large, emerald-green tubular eyes embedded deep within the skull. For decades, researchers only knew the barreleye from specimens damaged by trawl nets, whose fragile head shields had collapsed during retrieval. It was not until remotely operated vehicles operated by the [Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute](https://www.mbari.org/news/barreleye-fish-with-tubular-eyes-and-transparent-head/) recorded intact individuals in their natural habitat that marine scientists understood the canopy's true structure.

The barreleye’s vision is a specialized adaptation for hunting in dim waters. The bright green pigments in its lenses act as a biological filter, cutting through residual downwelling sunlight to pinpoint the faint bioluminescent glow of siphonophores and small jellies floating overhead. While early anatomical studies suggested these tubular eyes were fixed gazing upward, direct observations proved that the barreleye can rotate its eyes forward within the fluid dome when orienting its body to swallow prey.

What appear to be eyes on the front of its dark snout are actually nares—olfactory organs similar to nostrils. Beneath them lies a tiny mouth, well-suited for picking delicate organisms off siphonophore tentacles. Researchers hypothesize that the transparent dome shields its sensitive eyes from stinging nematocysts when stealing prey from colonial jellies.
