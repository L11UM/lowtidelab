In April 1815, Mount Tambora erupted in present-day Indonesia. It was the largest volcanic explosion in recorded human history, throwing so much ash into the upper atmosphere that global temperatures plunged the following year.

1816 became known as the "Year Without a Summer." Frost wiped out crops in June across North America and Europe. Food prices skyrocketed, and a catastrophic oat shortage swept across the continent.

When oats run out, horses starve. And when horses starve, nineteenth-century society loses its primary engine. People were forced to slaughter their draft animals for meat or simply watch them die. Getting around without walking suddenly became a massive logistical crisis.

Enter Karl Drais, a German inventor living through the food shortage. Seeing a world stripped of its horses, Drais realized humans needed a mechanical alternative to personal transport—one that didn't eat grain.

In 1817, he unveiled the *Laufmaschine*, or "running machine." It was a wooden frame resting on two in-line wheels. It had no pedals or chain; you sat on a leather saddle and pushed yourself along with your feet, gliding between strides. It looked absurd, but on decent roads, it moved at twice the speed of walking and cost nothing to feed.

We tend to think of major inventions as the result of steady, logical progress. But the ancestor of the bicycle wasn't born from an itch for leisure or engineering curiosity. It was forged in the middle of a sudden ecological collapse.

Drais’s invention was initially mocked as the "dandy horse" and briefly faded away once the weather recovered and horse populations bounced back. But the concept was out there. Decades later, other inventors added pedals, gears, and pneumatic tires, turning a disaster-born substitute into the most efficient vehicle ever made.

Sometimes progress isn't a straight line of inspiration. Sometimes it just takes a mountain exploding on the other side of the planet.
