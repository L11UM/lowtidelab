Whenever I’m genuinely stuck between two choices, I flip a coin. But I rarely follow what the coin tells me to do.

There’s an old Danish poet and mathematician named Piet Hein who wrote a short verse about this exact thing. His advice was simple: when you're faced with a tough choice, toss a coin. Not because it decides for you, but because in that brief second while the coin is hanging in the air, you suddenly realize what you're hoping for.

Philosophers have spent centuries obsessing over rational decision-making. We invent utility matrices, pros-and-cons lists, and weighted averages to force our chaotic desires into orderly spreadsheets. The 14th-century philosopher Jean Buridan famously pondered a hypothetical donkey placed precisely halfway between two identical stacks of hay. Lacking a rational reason to choose one over the other, the donkey starves.

We like to think we’re smarter than Buridan’s donkey, but modern life is basically a giant field of identical haystacks. Should you order the tacos or the bowl? Read this book or watch that film? Stay in or go out? When options are roughly equal in value, strict logic breaks down. Rationality requires a clear difference to weigh; when options are equivalent, reason paralyzes us.

That’s where the coin toss comes in—not as an oracle, but as a provocation.

When heads means Plan A and tails means Plan B, your brain skips the deliberate, analytical filtering. The moment the quarter lands on tails, a tiny flash of disappointment or relief hits your chest before your conscious mind even has time to formulate a justification.

If you feel a phantom sting of disappointment at tails, you actually wanted heads all along. Your subconscious had already picked a favorite; your analytical brain was just getting in the way with its silly pro-con lists.

The coin isn't making the choice. It's just forcing your gut to stop waiting on your brain.
