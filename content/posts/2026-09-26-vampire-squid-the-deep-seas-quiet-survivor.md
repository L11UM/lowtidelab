The vampire squid sounds like a predator assembled for a horror story. The real animal is stranger, and quieter. *Vampyroteuthis infernalis* is neither a true squid nor an octopus; it is the last living member of an ancient cephalopod lineage.

It lives mostly 600 to 900 meters below the surface, in the oxygen minimum zone. At those depths, oxygen is scarce and chasing prey would be an expensive way to eat. Instead, the vampire squid gathers marine snow: drifting flakes of dead plankton, mucus, and other organic matter sinking from brighter water above. Two long filaments collect the particles, then the animal draws them in and passes them toward its mouth with the help of its arms. [MBARI's species account](https://www.mbari.org/animal/vampire-squid/) describes this unusual diet and the low-energy life that makes the habitat workable.

Its defense is just as well matched to the dark. When threatened, it can pull its webbed arms over its body, turning a familiar outline into a confusing cloak. If danger continues, the arm tips flash blue-green light. It can also release a glowing fluid to distract a predator; unlike many cephalopods, it does not rely on a cloud of ink. The arm-tip light has been studied in a [peer-reviewed paper indexed by MBARI](https://doi.org/10.2307/1543231).

There is a lesson in its menu. Marine snow carries carbon from surface waters into the deep, and animals that feed on it help move that material through the ocean. The vampire squid is not a monster waiting in the dark. It is a patient part of the planet's longest-running delivery route.
