More than a thousand meters down, solar light ceases entirely, leaving the bathypelagic zone suspended in perpetual cold and pressure. Into the beam of our forward lights emerges *Stygiomedusa gigantea*, commonly known as the giant phantom jelly. First collected in 1899 and formally described in 1910, this massive scyphozoan remains exceptionally rare; over more than a century of oceanographic work, scientific records count only around a hundred encounters worldwide. The [Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute](https://www.mbari.org/animal/giant-phantom-jelly/) has documented the species fewer than thirty times across thousands of deep remotely operated vehicle dives.

The creature's proportions defy typical jellyfish morphology. While its umbrella-shaped bell can span roughly one meter across, it lacks the fringe of thin, stinging marginal tentacles typical of many near-surface medusae. In their place, *S. gigantea* features four ribbon-like oral arms that stretch up to ten meters (33 feet) in length. These ruffled appendages billow through the water column like heavy fabric. Biologists infer that the jelly drifts with minimal energy expenditure, trailing these sprawling arms to ensnare plankton, small fish, and other gelatinous organisms in an environment where encounters with prey are scarce.

Its deep, uniform coloration—a dark reddish-brown to purplish-crimson—is an adaptation to the optical properties of seawater. Red light waves are absorbed within the upper tens of meters of the ocean, so animals inhabiting the bathypelagic appear completely black against the water. When struck by bioluminescent flashes, the tissue absorbs the blue light rather than reflecting it back to predators.

Despite being a predator, *S. gigantea* also functions as an oasis. Remotely operated vehicles have observed the pelagic brotula fish *Thalassobathia pelagica* swimming in close association with the bell and navigating through the oral arms. As documented in reports shared by [MBARI](https://www.mbari.org/news/phantom-jelly-stygiomedusa-gigantea/) and records from [Smithsonian Ocean](https://ocean.si.edu/ocean-life/invertebrates/jellyfish-and-comb-jellies), this relationship appears to provide the small fish with protective shelter and opportunistic scraps in a vast water column void of physical landmarks.
